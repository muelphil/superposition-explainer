## Introduction

This article is an interactive explorable explanation of *superposition* — a geometric phenomenon that may explain how neural network embeddings represent far more features than their dimensionality would naively allow. Guided by the experimental framework of Anthropic's *Toy Models of Superposition* (Elhage et al., 2022), we build from geometric foundations to a toy autoencoder and guide the reader through progressive experiments. Rather than presenting superposition as a predefined concept, the article lets readers discover it through hands-on interaction with the accompanying visualizations, and then formally names and contextualizes the phenomenon.

## Embeddings, Features, and the Dimensional Bottleneck

### What are embeddings?

Neural networks cannot operate directly on symbolic concepts such as words, grammatical roles, or semantic properties. Instead, they transform information into **embeddings** — fixed-length vectors of real numbers that live in a high-dimensional **embedding space** (also called a **latent space**). The purpose of such a space is to provide a compact numerical representation in which useful properties of the input can be encoded and manipulated through mathematical operations. In language models, this transformation happens at every stage: tokens are mapped to vectors, and each subsequent layer transforms these vectors into new ones, all of the same fixed dimensionality. Throughout this article, we use the term **embedding space** in a broad geometric sense: a vector space in which the model stores and processes internal information.

Crucially, an embedding vector is not simply "meaning compressed into numbers." Different **directions** in the embedding space may correspond to different features of the input, and the component along each direction indicates how strongly that feature is present. This geometric picture — features as directions, activations as magnitudes along those directions — is foundational to the analysis that follows, and will be developed formally in the next section.

### Why embedding dimension matters

The internal vectors of a neural network have a **fixed dimensionality** — a hard architectural constraint. In transformer models, this is called the **embedding dimension** or **model dimension** $d_{\text{model}}$, and it determines how many coordinates are available at each layer to store and process information. Increasing the embedding dimension is expensive: in dense transformer architectures, a large fraction of the parameters reside in matrices whose size scales roughly with the **square** of the embedding dimension. Doubling the width therefore increases parameter count by roughly a factor of four in the dominant layers — and with it memory, training cost, and inference cost.

Modern large language models typically use embedding dimensions in the range of roughly **3072 to 12,288**. These are large vector spaces by human standards, but still far smaller than the number of concepts a model may need to represent. In practice, embedding dimension is not a free resource: model designers cannot simply keep adding dimensions for every property the model might need to represent.

## The apparent mismatch: far more possible features than dimensions

### What are features?

In mechanistic interpretability research, a **feature** refers to a direction or pattern inside a model's internal activations that corresponds to a recognizable property of the input. Some features are relatively concrete, such as "it is something in plural," "this is about a person," or "this is in past tense." Others may be abstract, distributed, or difficult to name. Importantly, a feature is **not necessarily a single neuron** — in modern neural networks, many features appear to be represented as directions in activation space rather than being stored cleanly in one coordinate.

Features are not directly observed ground truth. Models are trained end-to-end to minimize predictive loss, not to explicitly learn a predefined set of human-labeled concepts. Researchers can identify directions that correlate with meaningful concepts, but we do not have a complete list of the "true features" inside a large language model, nor do we know that the model's internal decomposition aligns perfectly with human categories. Significant research effort has gone into uncovering interpretable features inside trained models; projects such as [Neuronpedia](https://www.neuronpedia.org/) catalog discovered features for a small set of model and make them searchable, illustrating both the progress made and the sheer scale of the problem.

### The mismatch

The number of potentially useful features is vast — and fundamentally combinatorial. Consider just the English lexicon: the Oxford English Dictionary lists over **170,000 words in current use**, giving a sense of scale for how many distinct concepts exist even in a single domain. But features are not merely a flat list — they are fundamentally **combinatorial**. Properties like "is_plural," "refers_to_food," or "negative_sentiment" combine freely, and each combination constitutes a distinct feature. This means the space of potentially useful features grows combinatorially with the number of underlying properties, and at any given point many features are likely active simultaneously — far exceeding what any fixed number of embedding dimensions could accommodate with one axis per feature.

If a model has a few thousand hidden dimensions but may need to track a vastly larger number of potentially useful features, how can it do so? Empirically, large language models exhibit surprisingly rich behavior, suggesting that their internal representations do more than assign one feature to one axis. This article aims to provide an understanding of how models might deal with this mismatch. Rather than analyzing a full-scale language model — where architecture complexity and training data make it nearly impossible to isolate any single effect — we use a small, controlled model that lets us study the compression problem in isolation.

## A toy model for exploring the mismatch

We study this problem using the experimental setup described in Anthropic's *Toy Models of Superposition* (2022), built around an **autoencoder** — a simple neural network trained to reconstruct its input. The input first passes through a smaller **embedding layer** whose dimension is lower than that of the input, and the model then attempts to reconstruct the original input from this compressed embedding. Because the embedding dimension is smaller than the input dimension, the model cannot simply copy the data through — it is forced to learn how to compress many features into fewer dimensions, exactly the pressure we want to study. This gives us a controlled setting to explore how learned representations deal with the mismatch between features and available dimensions. The formal architecture, loss function, and training details are introduced in a later section.
